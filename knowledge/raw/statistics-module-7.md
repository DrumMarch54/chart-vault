## Sample size guidelines

- For small populations, a quick rule of thumb is to take around 10% of the population as your sample.
- For large populations, the benefit of going over about 1,000 participants is small for most statistical purposes.
- In public opinion research, about 1,000 people often gives a ±3% margin of error at 95% confidence, no matter if the total population is 100,000 or 10 million.
- Example: population 5,000, 10% = 500 (a reasonable sample). Population 300,000, 10% = 30,000, but only about 1,000 are needed for similar accuracy.

Binomial vs multinomial: binomial has 2 possible outcomes (yes/no, success/fail); multinomial more than 2. Our day vibration case has 31 categories, so it is multinomial, not binomial.

Why categories can swap in rank: if the differences in percent between categories are small (for example 3.5% vs 3.3%), random sampling error can easily make them swap order from one sample to the next. This is more likely when there are many categories, each category has a small proportion, and the sample size is small.

Gaining more accuracy (binomial case): increase the sample size (more participants reduce the margin of error); reduce variance by controlling other factors (same demographic mix, same conditions); use stratified sampling (split the population into relevant subgroups, such as age and gender, and sample proportionally from each); repeat measurements over time and take the average.

## Ranked lists

When we use ranked lists, we are focusing on a highly selective group, for example the world's richest individuals. The statistics then describe patterns within the elite of that field, which can reveal the mindset, traits or circumstances linked to reaching the very top. For instance, using the Forbes list, we can ask: what is the most common day vibration among the 100 richest, the 500 richest and the 1000 richest people in the world? Comparing these subsets can show whether certain day vibrations are disproportionately represented at the very highest levels. (Smaller subsets give more specific results, but also higher uncertainty, so differences need to be large to be conclusive.)

## Confidence

We can't ask everyone, so we take a sample. If the sample is representative of the population, we can calculate a confidence interval that shows the range where the true percentage for the whole population likely lies. For example, in an opinion poll with 1000 respondents, if a party has 20% support and the margin of error is ±2.5 percentage points, we can be 95% confident that their real support in the population is between 17.5% and 22.5%. This range is the confidence interval.

Counterintuitive fact: the width of the interval depends mainly on the sample size, not the size of the total population. Once a random sample is around 1000, the interval is typically 2-3% for large populations, no matter if the population is 100,000 or 10 million.

## Historical context: the Mars effect

Michel Gauquelin (13 Nov 1928), French psychologist and statistician, studied correlations between planetary positions and human achievement. In 1946 he began studies in psychology and statistics at the Sorbonne. He is best known for the Mars Effect, a claimed statistical correlation between elite athletes and the planet Mars being in certain "key sectors" (just past rising or culminating) at birth. The findings were controversial: some replications confirmed the effect, others did not. Disputes centered on dataset selection, athlete definitions and birth time accuracy. Critics often cited selection bias as a possible explanation.

Gauquelin research, key findings: initially Michel and Françoise Gauquelin aimed to test astrology with the expectation of disproving it. Using official French birth records, they compiled hundreds (later thousands) of charts. To avoid the difficulty of measuring subjective traits for large groups, they looked for correlations between planetary "key sectors" and occupations. Findings: Mars more frequent in elite athletes' charts; Jupiter more frequent in actors' charts; Saturn more frequent in doctors' and scientists' charts. Geoffrey Dean noted: "These results were consistent with Mars = activity, Jupiter = extroverted show, Saturn = inner thoughts, which more or less fitted with astrology." Later debate focused on whether these patterns were genuine or due to selection bias and dataset definitions.

Gauquelin's findings vs astrological doctrine: based on scientific statistics vs not; sectors of no significant importance vs houses of considerable importance; only key sectors have an impact vs every house has an impact and each differs in quality; no sudden change at the border vs sudden change at the border; so far only Mars, Jupiter, Saturn vs all planets work, even certain stars; maximum power before the axes vs maximum power on the axes.

Puzzles for astrology: why do the effects appear only for planetary positions in key sectors, not for signs or aspects? Why in traditionally weak positions (cadent houses) rather than strong ones? Why link to occupation and not to character traits? Why does the occupational effect vanish at low eminence, and sometimes weaken at the very highest eminence? Why do the results sometimes deviate from tradition (Moon instead of Mercury for writers)? Why only five planets (Moon, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn) and not the Sun or others? If isolated factors are as meaningless as some astrologers claim, why is there any effect at all? And why is the effect so small?

Riddles for science: why no correlation with physical variables such as planet size or distance? Why no correlation with the Sun? Why is eminence important? Why only an effect at birth, and why stronger at round clock times? Why does it disappear when birth is induced or surgically assisted? Why is it amplified when geomagnetic activity is high at birth, or when both parents have the same planet highlighted? If physical influences are constant, what is so special about the moment of birth rather than conception? And again, why an effect at all? What possible evolutionary advantage could it bring?

Debates on statistics in astrology. Peter Niehenke: "It is practically impossible to confirm astrology through statistical studies." The usefulness, value, interpretation and methodology of such studies remain a topic of ongoing debate; for every study that appears to support or refute astrology's legitimacy, both astrologers and skeptics often criticize its methods. Richard Vetter, strongly opposed to statistical validation of astrology: "Because of its quantitative view of numbers, statistics are most inadequate to test the truth of astrology." According to Vetter the core assumption of statistics, that random, unintended distributions are the norm, is opposed to astrology's view of meaningful patterns; statistics deliberately eliminate special conditions, but astrology sees individuality as the central building block of the world; in statistical testing the unique and exceptional are left out, while astrology places them at the center.

Even though statistics as a science would like to ignore small samples and disregard "unintended distributions", we still think they have a lot to say.
