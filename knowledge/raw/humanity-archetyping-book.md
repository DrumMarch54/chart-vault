### Does Sample Size Matter?

Sample size is important when we want to answer questions like: What is the most common birthdate among professional athletes?

To get a 100% accurate answer, we would need to include all professional athletes in the world. Estimates suggest that there are around 85,000 active professional athletes today across sports like football (soccer), American football, basketball, baseball, athletics, and others. Our database contains 21,788 athletes, which corresponds to roughly a quarter of that number.

However, our dataset spans a much longer time frame — primarily from around 1900 to 2000. A few entries fall outside this range, but they are statistically negligible. If we estimate the total number of professional athletes who lived during that period, the number might be closer to 300,000. This means our current dataset represents about 7% of that historical population.

How large does a sample need to be?

In statistics, the ideal sample size depends on several factors, including:

- The total population size

- The desired confidence level (commonly 95%)

- The acceptable margin of error

- The expected variability in the data For large populations, a sample of around 1,000 people is often sufficient to estimate proportions with a margin of error of ±3% at a 95% confidence level — if the sample is random and representative. This is why public opinion polls often use this number.

However, more data is always better when feasible. Larger sample sizes reduce the margin of error and make it easier to detect small but statistically significant patterns. In our case, we have more than 20,000 athletes, which makes our findings very robust — even if the sample isn’t perfectly random, the sheer size gives the data strong statistical value.

Why this matters Statistics doesn’t require us to study everyone to uncover meaningful patterns. Thanks to well-established principles — including sampling theory and the law of large numbers — we can draw valid conclusions from subsets of the population, as long as we account for limitations and biases.

So while it’s true that we don’t need hundreds of thousands of records to see trends, our large sample of over 20,000 athletes provides an excellent foundation for detecting correlations between birth numbers and career choices.

In some cases, it is actually preferable that the sample size is not too large and instead consists of a curated selection from a ranked list, because the statistics in that case reflect the best of the best.
